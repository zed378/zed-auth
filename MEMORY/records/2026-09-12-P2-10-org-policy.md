## Stored, validated, returned by the API, and read by nothing

`docs/PLAN/17`'s Phase 2 criterion is worded precisely: organization settings must be **enforced at login time, not merely stored**. The precision turns out to be load-bearing.

`password_policy` was enforced — `P1-02` reads it per organization on every password set and check, and `max_age_days` already forces a change at the next login.

`session_lifetime_hours` and `allowed_login_methods` were not. Both were accepted on write, validated against the schema, rejected unknown keys correctly, returned by the Management API, shown in the console — and **read by nothing at all**. Every organization got the handler's single twelve-hour default whatever its settings said, and an organization that excluded password sign-in got password sign-in.

The mutation output says it best. Reverting the fix produces:

```
an organization configured for 1 hour got a session lasting 11h59m59s
an organization configured for 720 hours got a session lasting 11h59m59s
```

That was the behaviour this morning.

## Checked before the password, not after

The login-method check runs **before** any credential work, and the ordering is deliberate in two directions.

It is not about the user. An organization that does not permit password sign-in does not permit it for anybody, so verifying a password in order to then refuse the method is work done to reach a conclusion already available.

And it keeps the refusal off the rate limiter. Counting it would let an organization's own configuration lock out its users' addresses — a denial of service delivered by a settings change, against people who did nothing. There is a test for that, because it is the kind of interaction nobody notices until an administrator tightens a policy and the support queue fills up.

## The one refusal that is allowed to say what is wrong

This file works hard to make every login refusal identical: a wrong password, an unknown address and a locked account produce the same bytes, because they differ only in facts about the **user** and telling them apart is an enumeration oracle.

`resultMethodNotAllowed` is different, and may be. It is a fact about the **organization**, which the person already knows they belong to — they arrived through one of its applications. Hiding it behind "incorrect email or password" would send somebody to reset a password that was never going to work, which is a worse outcome than the disclosure it avoids.

## Clamped, not refused and not honoured

A `session_lifetime_hours` of 100,000 is clamped to the ceiling, reported at `WARN`, and the login proceeds.

Refusing would mean one bad settings value stops every login in the organization — a typo in a form becoming an outage. Honouring it would mean a session outliving any policy anybody intended. The clamp is the third option, and the `WARN` is what stops it being silent: a policy an administrator believes is in force and is not should be discoverable from a log rather than by experiment.

## Tightening does not lock anybody out

Step 6 asks for the transition case to be designed rather than left emergent. The design, stated: `password_policy` governs what a **new** password must satisfy. The only thing that acts on an existing password is `max_age_days`, which `P1-02` deliberately enforces at the next login.

So tightening `min_length` to 64 does not invalidate anybody's password, and there is a test that sets exactly that and signs in afterwards.

## `mfa_required` stays stored and unenforced

Step 4 is explicit: store it, validate it, and do not advertise it as enforced before Phase 3 gives it something to enforce. It is stored as a `*bool` — so `false` and absent stay different intentions — and the console marks its Multi-factor tab **unavailable** rather than claiming anything, which is `CLAUDE.md`'s no-unshipped-claims rule holding without needing a change here.

## A smaller thing left undone

The login **page** still renders a password form for an organization that does not permit passwords; the refusal happens on submission. That is correct as a security boundary and poor as an interface — somebody types a password that was never going to work.

Fixing it means the page needs a state it does not have, which is a template change rather than a policy one. Named here rather than quietly shipped as done.
